--- title: Data and methodology description: Where every place, border, date and person on History Globe comes from, how it was checked, and what is still uncertain. ---

The globe is built from open scholarly datasets, corrected and extended by hand, and checked automatically on every build. This page explains where each layer comes from, what we changed and what we do not know.

Places of the Bible

The {{places}} places and their verses come from OpenBible.info’s Bible Geocoding Data, which weighs the identifications proposed in the literature for each place. We keep its preferred identification and its estimate of how likely each candidate site is.

Of these {{places}} places, {{named}} are named in a verse of the books OpenBible tags, and each of them has a page of its own; the other {{unnamed}} are records it keeps that no verse of those books names (Adasa of 1 Maccabees, for one). {{articles}} places have an article. {{excluded}} more were left out for want of a point that may be published (below), and {{without_point}} have no point in OpenBible at all.

  • Certainty in words. When no serious second site is proposed, OpenBible’s score (0–1000) indicates how secure the identification is: at 800 or above it is treated as settled and the card says nothing; below that, the card says most likely (600 or above) or tentative. A place is disputed when the candidates more than 5 km from the leading one together hold at least a fifth of the shares; all candidate sites are then shown with their shares.
  • No coordinates from OpenStreetMap or Google. OpenBible records where each coordinate came from. Points it took from OpenStreetMap or Google are replaced by Wikidata’s coordinates (CC0), or the place is left out ({{excluded}} places). A separate check fails the build if any such point remains.
  • Names from the text. Russian names follow the Synodal translation of 1876: for nearly every name the build keeps a verse in which it occurs and checks that the name is in that verse.

When places stood

A city on the map is drawn in full only in the years it stood. For {{life}} places of the Bible the years of founding, destruction and abandonment are recorded by hand, each with its sources (ancient authors, chronicles, inscriptions, excavation reports). Only firm dates are recorded: a town whose founding or end is disputed is given no year for it.

Of the {{ancient}} cities of the ancient world that the Bible does not name — capitals, ports and sanctuaries such as Ugarit, Hattusa, Mari or Delphi — {{ancient_hand}} were chosen and dated one by one: each is placed by its record in Pleiades or Wikidata, on the ancient site rather than the modern town, and dated from those records and encyclopaedic accounts, reconciled by hand. The other {{ancient_script}} were chosen by a script: settlements, sanctuaries and ports in Pleiades whose Wikidata item is an ancient site, not a modern town; their points are Pleiades’, their names Wikidata’s (where it has none in Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian or Serbian, ours, in the form that language’s classical scholarship uses; in the other languages, where it has none, the English one), and their years the span of the periods Pleiades attests them in, so all of them are marked “c.”. Where a town was destroyed in the middle of a period, its end is set by hand from the history. A place appears at a zoom set by how often the Bible names it; a town the Bible barely names but the world knows well — Carthage, Sparta, Ur — appears earlier, by the number of Wikipedia articles about the ancient site itself.

Borders

States and their borders year by year come from Cliopatria (Seshat Global History Databank, v0.2.0), an open dataset of the world’s polities from 3400 BC onwards. It is drawn from historical atlases and is approximate, especially before the first millennium BC.

For the lands of the Bible we correct it where the sources disagree with it: {{overrides}} corrections at present, each with its reason and its sources. Judah stays on the map until 586 BC, Israel begins with Saul’s reign in about 1050 BC rather than 1000 BC, Philistia is kept to the coastal plain of its five cities, and a dynasty ends when it loses its last seat of power. The Levant and Egypt, Mesopotamia, Anatolia and Iran, the Greco-Roman world of the New Testament and the Near East to AD 1300 have been checked year by year, among them Assyria’s decline and recovery in the eighth century BC, Babylon under Merodach-baladan, Pergamon left to Rome in 133 BC, Macedonia after Pydna and Thrace annexed in AD 46. Each batch of corrections is first tested against the shapes, then checked again against the sources in a second, independent review pass.

A kingdom that paid tribute to an empire, or ruled as its client, is drawn in a lighter shade of the empire’s colour, with “under Assyria” or “under Rome” beneath its name: Judah under Assyria, Egypt and Babylon; Israel and Philistia under Assyria; Hasmonean and Herodian Judea under Rome (and, for three years, Parthia); and the Nabataeans and Commagene under Rome. These {{vassals}} periods were drafted from the Assyrian royal inscriptions, the Babylonian Chronicles, the Bible, Josephus and modern histories, and checked again in a separate review pass; a year known only to within a decade is marked “c.” in the data. After Samaria fell in 722 BC, the land of Israel is drawn as the Assyrian province it became.

People

The {{people}} people of the Bible and their family links come from STEP Bible’s TIPNR (Tyndale House, Cambridge). We did not take the links on trust:

  • the family links of the {{n:1200}} people the text names most often, and the genealogies of 1 Chronicles, were read against the verses; 153 links the text does not state were removed — the brothers of Jesus as sons of Joseph and Mary (the churches read this differently), Jezebel as the mother of Ahaziah — and the missing links of the genealogy of Jesus were added from the text;
  • all {{n:1136}} links between a person and a place where they play a central or supporting role were read verse by verse; 344 were removed (Moses “in Canaan”, Abraham “in Sodom”, a son’s places given to his father) and 94 were given a better verse;
  • namesakes are told apart as the text tells them apart: “John the Baptist”, “John son of Zebedee”, “Ahaziah, king of Judah”.

Events, tours and articles

The {{events}} events, {{tours}} tours and {{articles}} articles are drafted by the author with the assistance of large language models and then checked in separate review passes against the sources: the biblical text for what it says, ancient authors, chronicles and inscriptions for dates, and the scholarly literature for sites. Every article, answer and tour lists its sources, for the text as a whole rather than sentence by sentence. Quotations come only from the public-domain Bible of each language, listed on the sources page. No tour has passed its first review unchanged; each was corrected and checked again. The last full pass, completed on 17 October 2026, re-read all 746 articles against their sources, about 210 of them twice, and made {{n:1088}} corrections, each carried into the 11 languages the site had then; outright errors were found in 157 articles. The five languages added on 24 October 2026 (Swedish, Norwegian, Danish, Tagalog and Serbian) and the five added on 26 October 2026 (Hungarian, Finnish, Vietnamese, Romanian and Bulgarian) were translated from the corrected texts; each of their texts was then read against the English in an independent review pass, and every quotation was checked against that language’s Bible. These passes are the project’s own checks, not peer review. Reviews by specialists are published under their names on the reviews page, and each article a specialist has reviewed names the reviewer and links to the review. Published so far: .

The battles and sieges ({{battles}}) are those the Bible tells, and a few outside it that decided Israel’s fate. Each stands at the place it was fought and shows on the map in its years. The Bible’s are dated as the tours date the same stories; the others carry no verse but an ancient or scholarly source. A second, independent review pass checked the draft against the Synodal text and the King James Version: of 58 entries (October 2026) 16 were corrected, and one was dropped, as the map has no point for its site.

The Maccabean events and battles cite 1–2 Maccabees, which the Synodal Bible carries and the Protestant canon does not: every such citation is marked “deuterocanonical”, and its link opens a text that has the book.

Distances and travel time

The distance to a stop is given in a straight line. From 312 BC, when the Roman roads begin, the way along the Itiner-e roads is given beside it: the shortest through their network, unless it is more than two and a half times the straight line; a leg sailed is not counted by road. Time on foot is reckoned at 30 km a day. Vegetius gives the Roman army’s ordinary pace as 20 Roman miles, about 29.6 km, in five summer hours (Epitome of Military Science 1.9), and the ORBIS model of the Roman world sets 30 km a day for travellers on foot (W. Scheidel, E. Meeks and J. Weiland, 2012). It is reckoned along the road where the leg has one, else along the straight line, which roads only lengthen, so that the tour pages mark it as a floor (“≥”); there is no allowance for weather, rests or loads.

At sea a voyage gives its days where the text does (Acts 20:6, “in five days”), with that verse, and as roughly as the text gives them; the map’s card gives only these. Elsewhere the tour pages add an estimate, marked as one, between two speeds from L. Casson’s study of ancient voyages (“Speed under Sail of Ancient Ships”, 1951): about 4 knots with a fair wind, the lower end of the 4 to 6 knots he found over open water (3 to 4 along coasts and among islands), and 2 knots against it, where he found “from less than 2 to 2½ knots”, so that the longer figure is not an upper bound; both day and night, over the straight line across the water, or along the waypoints a leg is given where that line would cross land. A ship that put in for the night, waited for a wind or rounded a cape took longer: the crossing between Troas and Neapolis took two days one way (Acts 16:11) and five the other (Acts 20:6). The times are of the days on the move, never of the stays. A leg that falls within days the text gives for a longer voyage (Fair Havens to Cauda, within the fourteen nights of Acts 27:27) gets no time of its own. Where the text crosses whole regions without the way (Acts 20:1–3, Ephesus through Macedonia and Greece to Philippi), the leg says so and gives no time. A leg’s relief is read from the Copernicus elevation data (through Mapterhorn) along that same road, or without one along the straight line; the caption under the profile says which.

Dates and chronology

The map shows every story in a year, so its dates follow one convention; where scholars disagree, the year is a point for the map, not a verdict. The dates of the early biblical story, before the kingdom divides, follow the text’s own chronology. That is a convention of the map, not a historical claim: much of this early history is disputed, and some of it is doubted altogether, as the answer to When was the Exodus? sets out.

  • The kings of Israel and Judah follow the chronology of E. R. Thiele (The Mysterious Numbers of the Hebrew Kings): Solomon begins the Temple c. 966 BC and the kingdom divides c. 931 BC. Other reconstructions differ by some years; W. F. Albright, for one, put the division in 922 BC.
  • Earlier dates are counted back from these as the text counts them: the Exodus in 1446 BC, 480 years before the Temple (1 Kings 6:1), the conquest in 1406 BC and David’s reign from 1010 BC. They are conventions: many scholars put the Exodus in the thirteenth century BC or doubt that it happened as told. The patriarchs stand at conventional years.
  • Events outside the Bible are dated by the records that fix them (the Babylonian Chronicle, the Assyrian annals, Josephus, a dated inscription), each with its sources, in most cases two or more; the fall of Jerusalem is shown at c. 586 BC (587 BC is also held).
  • The New Testament: the birth of Jesus c. 5 BC, the crucifixion c. AD 30 (AD 33 is also held), and Paul’s journeys at the years usually given.
  • “c.” marks a year known only approximately, as every year before the first millennium BC is.

Checks on every build

Nothing is published unless every check passes:

  • every source carries an allowed licence (no share-alike, no non-commercial data);
  • every reference points to a real book, chapter and verse;
  • every Russian name is found in the verse given for it;
  • every tour stop is named in its passage, or the exception is documented with its reason;
  • every correction to the borders and every date of a city has a source;
  • the content schemas are strict: an unknown or misspelt field stops the build.

Choices the map makes

Some choices behind the map are not neutral, and a reader of another tradition might make them differently. They are set out here.

  • Bible editions. Each language quotes one Bible, and only one in the public domain, so that its verses can be published in full. These are mostly editions of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and most of them are Protestant translations, which is what most languages have in the public domain; the Russian Synodal Bible and the Romanian Bible of 1914 are those of the Orthodox churches. No Catholic edition is quoted yet. The choice follows what may be published, not a confession; the sources page names each edition.
  • The canon. The places come from OpenBible, which covers the 66 books of the Protestant canon. 1–2 Maccabees are cited for the events of their time and marked deuterocanonical; Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach and Baruch, which the Catholic and Orthodox churches include in the Old Testament, are not yet on the map.
  • Names. Nearly every Russian name is checked against a verse of the Synodal text. The names in the other languages follow each language’s Bible as far as its glossary goes; they are not yet checked verse by verse, and native speakers have not yet reviewed them.
  • The early story. The early biblical story is dated by the text’s own chronology (above), and the kingdom of Saul, David and Solomon is drawn as the text describes it, although its extent is one of the most disputed questions of the period. The tours retell the stories as the text tells them: that a tour follows the text is not a claim that it happened as told.
  • Review. The texts were checked in the project’s own passes. Review by specialists has begun and so far covers a small part of them; each review is published on the reviews page.

What is uncertain

  • Dates before the first millennium BC are approximate, and some are disputed; the map marks them “c.”.
  • OpenBible’s shares between candidate sites are an estimate, not a scholarly consensus.
  • Ancient borders are drawn from modern atlases and simplify a reality of shifting control.
  • Many minor places still have no recorded years; they are drawn as standing throughout (or, for places named only in the New Testament, from New Testament times onwards).

How to cite

History Globe is corrected as it goes, so a citation names the date of the data it used. Every documentation page gives that date at its foot (“Data as of”), and the API’s index.json gives it as built_at.

The site:

Arnaut, Vadym. History Globe: an atlas of biblical history. {{site}}/
(data of {{built}}; accessed …).

A page, by its title and address:

“Jerusalem.” In Vadym Arnaut, History Globe. {{site}}/en/place/jerusalem/
(data of {{built}}; accessed …).

The data, by the date of the snapshot that the dataset’s card gives:

Arnaut, Vadym. History Globe dataset. Hugging Face, snapshot of ….
https://huggingface.co/datasets/ArVaViT/history-globe. CC BY 4.0.

Corrections

Found a mistake, a missing source or a better reading? Write to historyglobe.vadym@gmail.com; no account is needed. Name the page and what is wrong, with a source if you have one. Every report is read, and a confirmed error is corrected.

With a GitHub account you can also use the Found a mistake? link on every place card, which opens an issue in the public repository with the place filled in, or open one directly: github.com/ArVaViT/history-globe/issues.
